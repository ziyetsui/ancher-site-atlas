About the Reporter

How I Report

The story is almost never in the press release. It is in the appendix, the amended filing, the line item that does not add up. I start with public records — incorporation papers, court dockets, procurement logs, FOIA returns — and I read them the way an auditor reads a ledger: looking for the place where the numbers stop telling the truth.

A single document proves nothing. A name in a registry is a lead, not a finding. So everything gets a second source — a corroborating filing, a check that cleared, a person who was in the room. If it cannot survive a lawyer's read, it does not run. That discipline is slow, and it is the whole job.

Follow the moneyMoney leaves a trail even when people do not. Shell companies have registered agents. Contracts have signatures. Payrolls have totals that have to reconcile. Trace the dollars long enough and they stop being abstractions — they become a decision, made by someone, on a date, with a paper to prove it.

I write for editors who need it nailed down, and for readers who are owed the plain version of what happened. No insinuation, no anonymous shrugs — just the records, the names, and what the two of them together can be made to say.

Tips matter. The best stories of my career started with a stranger who noticed something was wrong and decided not to let it go. If that is you, the door is below.

The Rules of the Desk

Standards
1

One document is a lead, not a finding.

Nothing runs on a single source. Every claim gets a second filing, a cleared check, or a person who was in the room — or it stays in the notebook.

2

If a lawyer can't read it, the reader can't either.

Every line has to survive a hostile read before it survives an edit. Insinuation is not reporting; the records have to carry the weight.

3

The subject hears the worst of it first.

No one is named in a story before they've had every allegation, in detail, with time to answer. Surprises belong in the lede, not in the right-of-reply.

4

Protect the source above the scoop.

A tip is a trust. I'd kill a story before I'd burn the person who brought it. Signal, encrypted mail, and a clear record of what was promised.

Questions from the Desk

Before You Write

How do you protect a source?

Signal or encrypted mail, no notes that name you, and nothing promised I can't keep. I've gone to the mat to protect tippers before, and I will again.

Do I need proof to send a tip?

No. A hunch, a document, or a number that looks wrong is enough to start. The reporting is my job — your job is to point.

Will my name be in the story?

Only if you want it there, on the record, with your consent. Most sources are never named, and that is by design.

Can editors commission a piece?

Yes — I take select freelance investigations on contract. Reach the desk below and I'll tell you honestly what's reportable.
