at the drawing table

How I make a little book

600+ kids drew their own animal with me — 3 cities, all sold out 🐙
“A picture book is a tiny theatre you hold in two hands. I draw it slowly, so a child can stay a while.”

I have spent twelve years drawing by hand — ink first, then watercolour, then the bits of crayon and coloured pencil that sneak in. Nothing on this page was made by a machine, and I’d like to keep it that way.

Every story starts as a scribble in a battered sketchbook: an otter posting a letter, a pine tree that refuses to hurry. I let the drawing tell me what the words can’t. By the time a manuscript reaches a publisher, the characters already know how they walk.

Between books I run “Draw Your Own Animal” workshops — three cities so far, every one sold out, 600-plus small illustrators and a heroic amount of glitter. Watching a six-year-old decide their creature has seven legs is the best research there is.

…so let’s make something together.

Four things I draw by

  1. Leave the wobble in.

    A line that shakes a little is a line a hand actually made. I don’t straighten it out — that wobble is where the warmth lives.

  2. Draw slowly, so a child can stay.

    A picture book is a tiny theatre held in two hands. I fill the corners, so there’s always one more thing to find on the fortieth bedtime.

  3. Let the picture say what the words can’t.

    The best page leaves room. If the drawing already shows the sadness, the sentence doesn’t need to name it.

  4. No machine on this page.

    Every mark here was inked or painted by hand. It’s slower, it’s messier, and I’d like to keep it exactly that way.
