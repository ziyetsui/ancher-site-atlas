On Writing

A film tells you what it wants you to feel. My job is to notice what it is doing to get there — and to say it plainly enough that you can’t un-see it.

I read a film the way you’d read a paragraph that’s trying to convince you of something. Where does it slow down? What does it cut away from? Which face does it trust you to remember? Fifteen years in, I’ve become less interested in whether a movie is good and more interested in what it’s training us to want.

That became a book — The Attention We Pay — about how the movies we watch quietly rehearse the way we look at everything else. The criticism is the same project at a smaller scale: one film, one moment, held up to the light of the week it arrived in. If that’s the kind of writing your pages need, I’d like to hear what you’re thinking about.

iii.

How I Read a Film

  1. The cut is the argument.

    Where a film chooses to look away tells you more than what it shows. I follow the edit before I follow the plot.

  2. Describe before you judge.

    Good or bad is the least interesting thing a sentence can say. First make the reader see the shot; the verdict can take care of itself.

  3. Take the audience seriously.

    People notice more than they can name. Criticism is the work of naming it — plainly, without flattering the writer or the reader.

  4. The film arrived in a week.

    No movie exists in a vacuum. I hold each one up to the light of the moment it was released into, and ask what it was for.
