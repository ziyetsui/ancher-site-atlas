What I hold to

A short list of things I refuse to compromise on.

After a decade shipping consumer software, these are the working beliefs that survived contact with real teams and real deadlines.

  • Start from the user's worst day, not their best.

    The flows people remember are the ones that failed gracefully—the empty state, the lost connection, the accidental delete. I design those first.

  • A demo is a promise; ship it or cut it.

    If a feature is good enough to show in a review, it's worth the work to make it real. Vaporware erodes trust faster than a missed quarter.

  • Defaults are the design.

    Most people never open settings. The choices baked into the out-of-box experience are the ones that actually shape behavior.

  • Critique the work, protect the person.

    Strong opinions about the artifact, generous assumptions about the maker. I've never seen harshness produce better craft.

Operating principles

Five rules I run my practice by.

Numbered so I can point to them in a 1:1 and so a new teammate knows what to expect on week one.

  1. 01

    Show the rough draft early

    I'd rather be wrong in week one than polished and wrong in week six. Early ugliness invites the feedback that matters.

  2. 02

    Name the trade-off out loud

    Every decision spends something—time, scope, or simplicity. I write down what we're paying so future me isn't confused.

  3. 03

    Measure twice, then trust the gut

    Data sets the boundaries of the room. Taste decides where the furniture goes. Both, in that order.

  4. 04

    Leave the campsite cleaner

    Every file I touch gets a little tidier—one renamed variable, one deleted dead path. Quality compounds quietly.

  5. 05

    Default to writing it down

    A decision that lives only in a meeting didn't happen. If it matters, it goes in a doc someone can find later.
